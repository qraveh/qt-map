**The idea.** Thousands of rf-SQUID flux qubits (`fluxq`) serve as programmable Ising spins: a loop's persistent current, one way or the other, is the spin; inductive couplers set the interactions; the chip evolves continuously (`g_anneal`) from a transverse-field ground state toward that of the programmed problem. The problem is static: biases are held on chip, the answer latched as bits. Table 8.3's bet, special-purpose scale now, is a trade: count and connectivity for gates, logical qubits and any route to fault tolerance.

**What it needs.** The qubits, the long couplers' multilayer routing (`cx_lr`) and the SFQ flux DACs (`ct_fluxdac`) all *require* one superconducting process (`fab_sc`). Readout (`ro_fluxro`) *requires* the qubits and the DACs: a quantum flux parametron latches each qubit's current, on-chip shift registers carry the bit out. Static biases let DACs control "tens of thousands of qubits and couplers with just 200 bias wires" [C][A8]. The empty layers mean: no encoding, so a qubit is a Hamiltonian term, not a memory; no readout during the anneal, so no syndrome for a code or decoder. Errors are never detected, only averaged over samples: more qubits buy larger problems, not better answers.

**Where it stands.** Three machines, two devices, 14 of 32 cells documented (Table 8.1), no gate. Advantage2 (D-Wave, May 2025; 4,400+ qubits, Zephyr 20-way, 12.5 kW [C][A1]) supersedes Advantage (2020). D-Wave's "beyond-classical" spin-glass result (Science, March 2025) was substantially eroded within weeks by tensor-network and variational methods [D][A2]; D-Wave maintains its largest instances remain unmatched [P][A3]. Qilimanjaro's coherent annealer shows no device, count or benchmark. §2.8 scores only E 4 and F 2; H1-2026 revenue fell 67% [C][A4]; a CHIPS agreement (up to $100 M, 8 September 2026) funds work toward a 100,000-qubit annealer and a gate-model system [C][R][B19].

**Exceptions and borrowings.** No cell falls off the slots; all fit here best. Both Advantage machines list dispersive readout (`ro_disp`), which the map says *replaces* the flux latch, as an alternate: perimeter resonators read the shift-register bits after the latch, so the two sit in series: a hybrid, not rivals. Qilimanjaro's coupler and DAC cells are inferred, its readout undisclosed: a data gap. Borrowings run outward: the D-Wave gate-model dual-rail system takes the flux DACs as an alternate, and IBM Quantum Loon and its successors use the long couplers for qLDPC checks.

**The next test.** §5.2 has no annealing row; D-Wave's roadmap serves. Its 20,000-qubit system for 2029 and 100,000 qubits by 2031 [R][A4] rest on "superconducting interconnect technologies" for "multi-chip fabrics" [C][A4], the layer the architecture leaves empty. Confirmation by March 2028: a multi-chip anneal with couplers across chip boundaries, or a beyond-classical claim surviving a year of classical challenge. Demotion: neither by then, and Advantage3 moved or dropped.

**What the builders say.** D-Wave's Advantage2 has more than 4,400 qubits, each coupled to 20 others so that larger problems can be embedded [C][A9]. D-Wave says an Advantage2 prototype simulated magnetic materials in minutes where a classical supercomputer would need nearly a million years, a result published in Science [D][A10]. It plans a multi-chip Advantage3: 20,000 qubits in 2029 and 100,000 by 2031 [R][A11], wired through a new input-output design needing at most 20% more lines than today's 4,500-qubit machine [C][A11]. Qilimanjaro builds analog processors from fluxonium qubits, which it calls error-resilient by design and usable before fault tolerance; the first, 10 qubits, runs at the Barcelona Supercomputing Center, with two more generations due in 2026 and 2027 [C][A12].
